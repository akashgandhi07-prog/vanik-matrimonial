Before saying yes, talk openly about the things that shape daily married life: values, family roles, where you will live, money, faith, children and careers. You are not looking for identical answers, you are looking for answers you can both live with, and a partner who can discuss differences calmly.

## When should you start asking the big questions?

Not on the first meeting. A first conversation is for finding out whether you enjoy each other's company. But do not leave the serious topics until after a formal engagement either, when it feels too late to change course.

A gentle rhythm works for many couples. Light topics in the first one or two meetings, then values and family once you both want to keep going, and practical matters like money and living arrangements before families become formally involved. If you meet through an introduction service like the [Vanik Matrimonial Register](/how-it-works), you both already know the other is looking for marriage, which makes it easier to be direct.

## How do you ask without it feeling like an interview?

Share your own answer first. "I've always imagined living near my parents, at least for the first few years. How do you see it?" feels very different from "Where do you want to live?"

Ask one big question per meeting, not ten. Listen more than you talk. If an answer surprises you, ask "Tell me more about that" before reacting. And remember it is fine for either of you to say "I haven't really thought about that yet".

## Values and day-to-day life

- What does a good weekend look like to you?
- How do you handle stress or a bad day at work?
- What are you proud of, and what are you still working on?
- How do you prefer to resolve disagreements: talk it through straight away, or take some space first?
- What does respect look like to you in a marriage?
- How do you feel about sharing housework and cooking?

That last one sounds small, but expectations about who does what at home are worth discussing honestly, especially if you grew up in households that did things differently.

## Family and the wider parivar

- How close are you to your parents and siblings, and how often do you see them?
- What role do you expect your parents to play in our decisions?
- How would you handle it if your family and I disagreed about something?
- Are there family responsibilities I should know about, such as caring for a parent or supporting a sibling?
- How involved do you want our families to be in the wedding, and in married life afterwards?

There is no single right model. Some couples are very happy living close to both families and sharing a lot; others want more independence. What matters is that you both know what you are agreeing to.

## Where will you live?

- Do you hope to live with your parents, near them, or independently?
- Which towns or cities would you be happy to live in? Which would be hard?
- Would either of us relocate for work, or for the other person?
- Do you plan to stay in the UK long-term, or might we move abroad?

If you live in different cities, this question deserves time early on. Our guide on [finding a match beyond London](/guides/matches-beyond-london) looks at relocating for marriage in more depth.

## Money and finances

Money can feel awkward to raise, but it is far easier before marriage than after.

- How do you feel about joint versus separate accounts?
- Are you a saver or a spender, honestly?
- Do you have debts, such as student loans, a car loan or credit cards?
- Do you support family members financially, or expect to in future?
- What are your hopes around buying a home, and when?
- How would we make big spending decisions?

You do not need to share bank statements on a third date. But before you commit, you should each have a clear picture of the other's situation and habits.

## Faith and traditions

- How important is faith in your daily life?
- Which festivals, fasts or rituals matter to you?
- Do you visit a mandir or derasar regularly?
- What are your food practices at home and when eating out?
- How would we bring up children in terms of faith and culture?
- How do you feel about Gujarati being spoken at home?

If one of you comes from a Hindu family and the other from a Jain family, or one is more observant than the other, these conversations matter even more. Our guide to [Hindu and Jain matches](/guides/hindu-jain-matches) offers practical ways to talk about diet, faith and family.

## Children

- Do you want children? If so, roughly when, and how many?
- How would we share parenting and childcare?
- How do you feel about grandparents helping with childcare?
- What would we do if we found it hard to have children?
- What values would you most want to pass on?

This is one area where a real mismatch is hard to compromise on, so it is kind to both of you to be honest.

## Career and ambition

- Where do you see your career in five years?
- Would you consider changing jobs or cities for a partner?
- How do you feel about one partner working part-time or taking a career break?
- How many hours do you typically work, and does that suit you?
- What does a fair balance between work and home look like to you?

## What if you disagree on something important?

Disagreement is normal. Two people from different homes will see some things differently. Pay attention to how the conversation goes, not just the answer. Can you both explain your view without getting defensive? Is there room to find a middle way? Does the other person seem genuinely interested in what you think?

A difference you can talk about calmly is usually manageable. A difference that one person refuses to discuss is a much bigger warning sign.

## Should parents be part of these conversations?

Parents often have wisdom and good questions, and many families like to meet early. But the core conversations are between the two people getting married. Talk things through as a couple first, then involve families. If you are a parent reading this, our [guide for parents](/guides/parents-guide) has ideas on supporting without taking over.


## Frequently asked questions

### How many times should you meet before agreeing to marry?

There is no set number. What matters is that you have talked honestly about values, family, where you will live, money, faith and children, and that you have seen how you both handle a disagreement. For most couples that takes a good number of meetings and conversations over several months, not a handful of dates.

### What should you ask at a first meeting after a matrimonial introduction?

Keep the first meeting light: work, family, how you spend weekends, travel and food, and what made each of you start looking now. Save the bigger questions for once you both want to keep meeting. Our guide to [your first meeting](/guides/first-meeting) has more ideas.

### What are the red flags to look out for before marriage?

Be cautious if someone refuses to discuss important topics, becomes angry or dismissive when you disagree, pushes you to decide quickly, or asks for money. A difference you can talk about calmly is usually manageable; a refusal to talk is a much bigger warning sign. Our [staying safe](/guides/staying-safe) guide lists more.

### Is it rude to ask about money before marriage?

No, as long as you ask kindly and at the right stage. It is not a first-meeting topic, but before you commit you should each understand the other's debts, saving habits and any family support they give or expect to give. Sharing your own situation first makes it easier.

### What questions do families usually ask each other?

Families often want to understand values, faith practice, food at home, family responsibilities and where the couple might live. It works best when the couple have talked these through first and then bring their families in. Our [guide for parents](/guides/parents-guide) has ideas for supporting without taking over.
