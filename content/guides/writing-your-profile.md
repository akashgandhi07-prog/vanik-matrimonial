A matrimonial profile gets replies when it is specific, honest and warm: a clear recent photo, a few real details about your life, and a sense of what you hope for. You do not need to sound impressive. You need to sound like a real person someone would like to meet for a coffee.

## What does someone notice first?

Most people glance at a profile for a few seconds before deciding whether to read on. On the Vanik Matrimonial Register, a profile covers your education, job title, height, diet, religion, hobbies and photos, plus your future settlement plans if you choose to add them. That is a useful structure to think with, whichever service you use.

Get the basics right before you worry about anything clever. Accurate height, an honest diet choice and a photo that looks like you today will do more for you than a beautifully written paragraph.

## How should I describe my job and education?

Be plain and precise. Use a job title people recognise. "Associate, TMT" means little to most readers, while "Solicitor" is instantly clear. "Software Engineer" is better than a long internal title from your company's org chart.

If you are studying, between jobs or changing career, say so simply. "Final-year medical student" or "Retraining as a primary school teacher" reads as confident and honest. Nobody expects everyone to have a neat, finished career story in their twenties or thirties.

For education, give the subject and the place, for example "BSc Mathematics, University of Manchester". You do not need to list every qualification since GCSEs.

Do not stretch the truth. If the person you meet hears a different story over coffee, it creates doubt about everything else.

## What should I write about my hobbies?

This is the part most people rush, and it is where you can stand out. "Travelling, food, music, family" describes almost everyone. Pick two or three things you genuinely do and add one small detail to each.

Instead of: "I like travelling and cooking."

Try: "I try to visit one new city a year (Lisbon was the latest), and I am slowly learning to make my nani's dhokla without phoning her for help."

Details give the other person an easy first question. Someone reading that line can ask about Lisbon, or about the dhokla, and the conversation has already started. Keep it short, though. A few vivid lines beat a long list.

A few prompts if you are stuck:

- What do you do on a free Sunday?
- What have you got better at in the last year?
- What do your friends rely on you for?
- What would you happily talk about for an hour?

## Which photos work?

Photos matter, so give them some thought:

- **Main photo:** recent, head and shoulders, face clearly visible, in good natural light. Look relaxed, or smile if it comes easily.
- **No group photos as your main picture.** Nobody should have to guess which one is you. The register does not accept group shots or unclear images as a main photo.
- **Use all the slots you have.** The register allows up to three photos. A good mix is one clear face shot, one full-length, and one of you doing something you enjoy.
- **Ask a friend or sibling to take them.** They are almost always better than arm's-length selfies.
- **Skip heavy filters, sunglasses and photos from years ago.** If you have changed your hair, glasses or beard, update your pictures.
- **Dress as you might for a family function or a smart-casual dinner.** You want to look like yourself on a good day.

## How do I talk about diet and faith honestly?

Diet matters in many Gujarati households, and it is better to be clear early than to discover a mismatch later. The register asks you to choose veg, non-veg, vegan, Jain or pescetarian. Pick the one that reflects how you actually eat, not how you think you ought to.

Real life often has nuance. You might be vegetarian but eat eggs, or follow Jain food practice at home but be more relaxed when eating out. Be ready to say so openly in your first conversation. Our guide to [Hindu and Jain matches](/guides/hindu-jain-matches) covers how to talk about this kindly.

## Should I say what I am looking for?

Yes, briefly, and in terms of character and lifestyle rather than a checklist. "Someone kind, curious and close to their family" tells a reader more than a list of requirements.

The future settlement plans section is worth filling in. Lines like "Happy to stay in the UK", "Open to relocating within the UK" or "Would like to live within reach of my parents in Leicester" save both sides time. If location is a big question for you, read our guide on [finding a match beyond London](/guides/matches-beyond-london).

Avoid long lists of musts, such as minimum heights or salaries. They tend to read as demanding and can put off people who would have been a lovely fit.

## What should I leave out?

- **Negativity.** No complaints about past relationships, other apps or "time-wasters".
- **Identifying details.** Do not put your phone number, home address or a workplace that pinpoints you in your profile text. On the register, contact details are shared only inside the member dashboard when someone requests them.
- **Empty phrases.** "Simple person", "fun-loving" and "family-oriented" mean little on their own. Show it with a detail instead.
- **Writing by committee.** Parents and friends are wonderful proofreaders, but the voice should be yours. The person who replies wants to meet you, not your mum's description of you.

## A quick checklist before you submit

- Would a stranger learn three specific things about me?
- Is my main photo recent, clear and just me?
- Are my height, diet and religion accurate?
- Is there an easy question someone could ask me?
- Have I read it aloud once to check it sounds like me?


## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a matrimonial profile be?

Shorter than most people think. A few clear lines about your work, two or three hobbies with a small detail each, and a sentence on what you are hoping for is plenty. Someone should be able to read it in under a minute and come away with an easy question to ask you.

### How many photos should I put on a matrimonial profile?

Use every slot you have. On the Vanik Matrimonial Register you can add up to three photos, and a good mix is one clear head-and-shoulders shot, one full-length photo and one of you doing something you enjoy. Your main photo should be recent and show only you.

### Should I mention my salary on a matrimonial profile?

Usually not. A clear job title tells people enough about your work, and a salary in your profile can make it read like a negotiation. Money is worth talking about honestly once you are getting to know someone, and our guide to [questions to ask before saying yes](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-marriage) covers how.

### Can my parents write my matrimonial profile for me?

They can help, but the words should be yours. Parents are great at remembering the things you undersell and at proofreading, but the person reading your profile wants to meet you. On the Vanik Matrimonial Register the person looking to marry creates their own account and profile.

### How do I write a matrimonial profile if I have been married before?

Be honest and brief. A short, factual line is enough, without going into detail, and it is fairer to mention it up front than to raise it later. Then let the rest of your profile show who you are now and what you are hoping for.

### What should I write if I don't know what to say about myself?

Answer a few simple prompts: what you do on a free Sunday, what your friends rely on you for and what you could happily talk about for an hour. Pick the two or three answers that feel most like you and add one specific detail to each. Asking a sibling or close friend how they would describe you helps too.
