Hindu and Jain matches can work very well when both people talk honestly and early about food at home, faith practice, festivals and family expectations. Gujarati Hindu and Jain families often share a great deal of culture, so the differences are usually about detail and degree, and those are easier to agree on before an engagement than after.

## How similar are Gujarati Hindu and Jain families?

In many ways, very. Many families share a language, food, festivals like Diwali and New Year, family structures and a strong sense of community. Some Gujarati families have Hindu and Jain relatives on different branches of the same family tree, and plenty of households have a mix of practices.

The differences that matter most in a marriage are usually practical: what is cooked at home, which fasts and festivals are kept, how often you visit a mandir or derasar, and what each family expects day to day. Those can vary as much between two Jain families, or two Hindu families, as between a Hindu and a Jain family.

So the real question is not "Hindu or Jain?" but "how do we each live, and how do we want to live together?"

## Why is diet the first conversation to have?

Food is where faith shows up three times a day. A few things to be clear about:

- **Vegetarian, Jain or something else?** A Jain diet typically avoids root vegetables such as onion, garlic, potato and carrot, as well as meat, fish and eggs. Some people also avoid eating after sunset or keep particular food rules on certain days. A vegetarian Hindu diet may include onion and garlic, and some people eat eggs.
- **At home versus eating out.** Many people keep a stricter diet at home and are more flexible in restaurants, or the other way round.
- **During festivals and fasts.** Paryushan, Navratri, Ekadashi and other occasions can involve different food practices for different families.
- **For children.** What would you cook for your children, and would they follow one practice or have a choice?

The Vanik Matrimonial Register asks each member to choose a diet: veg, non-veg, vegan, Jain or pescetarian. That gives you a starting point, but a single word can hide a lot of nuance, so ask rather than assume.

A gentle way in: "I keep Jain food at home but I'm fairly relaxed when I'm out with friends. What's it like in your family?"

## How do you talk about faith without it becoming a debate?

Talk about practice and meaning rather than doctrine. Useful questions include:

- Which festivals feel most important to you, and how does your family celebrate them?
- Do you visit a mandir or derasar regularly, or mostly for special occasions?
- Do you fast? If so, when and how strictly?
- Is there a practice you would love to keep in your own home?
- How would you feel about joining in with my family's traditions, and me with yours?

Listen for warmth and openness. Two people with different levels of observance can build a very happy home if each respects what matters to the other. Difficulties tend to arise when one person expects the other to change without discussion.

## What about family expectations?

Families on both sides may have hopes about how the couple will live. These are worth talking about, gently and early:

- Will a new daughter-in-law or son-in-law be expected to adopt the other family's food practice at home?
- Which festivals will be spent with which family?
- Are there religious ceremonies the families will expect at the wedding?
- How will questions about raising children in either tradition be decided?

It helps to agree your own position as a couple before discussing it with parents. Families are usually reassured when they see two people who have thought things through together and are united. ## Is it fine to want a partner from your own tradition?

Yes, absolutely. Some people feel strongly that they want a partner who shares their exact practice, and that is a perfectly reasonable preference. Others are open to a Hindu or Jain partner as long as diet and values align. Both approaches are valid.

## Practical tips for mixed Hindu and Jain couples

- **Agree a home kitchen.** Many couples settle on a kitchen that suits the stricter diet, which means nobody is ever uncomfortable eating at home.
- **Learn each other's festivals.** Ask to join a Paryushan pratikraman or a family Navratri garba. Showing interest means a lot.
- **Have a plan for family meals.** Decide in advance how you will handle food at large gatherings so neither of you feels caught in the middle.
- **Talk about children early.** It is easier to agree principles before children arrive than to negotiate them later.
- **Keep talking.** Practice can change over time. Someone may become more observant after a family event, or less so after a move. Check in with each other.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a Jain marry a Hindu?

Yes. Gujarati Hindu and Jain families share a great deal of culture, and many happy marriages bring the two together. What matters most is agreeing early on food at home, which festivals and fasts you keep, and how you will involve both families.

### Do Jains eat onion and garlic?

Many Jains do not. A Jain diet typically avoids root vegetables such as onion, garlic, potato and carrot, as well as meat, fish and eggs. Practice varies from person to person and family to family, so it is always worth asking rather than assuming.

### What is the difference between a Jain diet and a vegetarian diet?

A vegetarian diet avoids meat and fish, and may include onion, garlic, other root vegetables and sometimes eggs. A Jain diet is usually stricter: it also avoids root vegetables, and some people avoid eating after sunset or keep extra rules on certain days.

### Will I have to follow a Jain diet if I marry into a Jain family?

Not necessarily. It is something to agree as a couple and with both families. Many couples keep a home kitchen that suits the stricter diet so everyone can eat at home comfortably, while each person keeps their own habits when eating out.
